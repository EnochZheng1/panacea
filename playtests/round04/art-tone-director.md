# Panacea · round 4 · playtest by "Art and Tone Director"

A veteran game art director and historian of the American illustrated press, 1880–1910. I judge production polish, whether the art direction holds, period accuracy, how well the humour lands, and motion.

**Verdict:** The paper is now the best thing in the game, and it tells the truth. In seven heavy-game editions I saw seven different leads. "THE SHELF BARE ON MULBERRY STREET" was followed by "STILL NO BOTTLES TO BE HAD", the correct period follow-up form. The columns end within a few lines of each other, and the standing matter is well-researched period journalism with good jokes. The counter is no longer a prototype: 21 cuts give a row of five different people. Two things keep the build from a finished look. First, the re-cut seals never reached the screen. The game still prints the round-3 seals, with upside-down lower legends, because the in-game cut files were not rebuilt. Second, the new hyphenation breaks words in the wrong places ("info-/rmed", "Fri-/edrich", "adve-/rtising", "purcha-/ses"), and a compositor of 1885 would have been fired for it.

## What works (keep it)

- **The leads now tell the truth, in period forms.** "THE SHELF BARE ON MULBERRY STREET / Callers Turned Away. / … / The Proprietor Cannot Brew Fast Enough." and then "STILL NO BOTTLES TO BE HAD / The Shortage Continues." is exactly the follow-up ladder a Bowery weekly would run. "A BOTTLE.—Not to be had for love or money." is a perfect caption. Round 3's finding 1 is fixed.
- **The exposed bribe is the best new copy.** "THE CORONER'S ENVELOPE / A Bought Verdict Comes to Light." "Coroner Nugent declines to discuss it, and is understood to have spent it. The verdict stands; the proprietor's name, which the envelope was meant to keep out of it, does not." "A GENTLEMAN WHO PAID THE CORONER'S FEE." The joke is on the seller and the coroner, which is exactly the target.
- **The standing matter is real period journalism.** The pedestrian match at the Garden (six-day go-as-you-please, 1879–88), Harry Hill's sparring, the Guion steamer _Wyoming_ from Liverpool, the Third Avenue El and the Signal Service forecast are all correct for 1885. The humour lands on the trade: "LOST.—…Also his health, which he believes he left at a druggist's." "…no remedies in the room." "WANTED.—A strong boy to carry bottles. Must not drink from them." "…not the Dr. —— of Mulberry street, and has never been." "The Gazette prints on cash terms, like the coroner." Keep all of it, and write more (finding 3).
- **The make-up balances.** The column feet fall within about 75–95 px in most editions, against 300–400 px in round 3. Folding five inquests under "THE MULBERRY STREET BOTTLES.—" is a correct run-in head.
- **The proof's strike-outs.** "~~A NEW PATRON.—Mrs. Bridget Halloran.~~ dele" is a real proofreader's gesture and makes the proof read as a working sheet.
- **The counter as a row.** Five different people with different hats, beards, pipes, veils and silhouettes. Death sways beside whoever is at risk, or waits faintly at the far end. The old-gentleman cut serves the well-to-do, and the proprietor is gone from the pool. "MR. LARSEN.—From a stock likeness." pays off the stock-cut joke.
- **The manicules.** "☞ the books", "☞ one inquest unanswered…", "TO THE PROOF ☞". The printer's fist is correct for the period and replaces the last modern arrows.
- **The still drawer prints the framed card.** It has its ruled ground and frame, the copper is modelled down the belly, and the receiver fills over the burn (`fire2_strip.png`).
- **Weather and markets.** "Mild. The well recover, regrettably." "Raw. Coughs ripening nicely." "Fog. Good for business." Morphine is $2.50 the ounce in THE MARKETS, now a plausible 1885 price current. "Lilies, scarce." follows the deaths.
- **Accuracy kept:** "NEW-YORK", the volume turning XV → XVI in January, numbers advancing by four, and "Terms: cash on delivery." on Hobart's order blank.
