# Panacea · round 9 · playtest by "The Satire Reader"

A reader of satire and dark comedy, here for the writing. I had never seen the game. I read every line to see who each joke is aimed at: the proprietors, the quacks, the press and the authorities, or the poor, the sick, immigrants and the dead.

**Verdict:** This is the best-written comic newspaper I have read in a game. Nearly all of it punches up, and it is often tied to the real month: Grant's death in the 25 July 1885 edition, Jumbo in September 1885, Waring's White Wings and Roosevelt's night patrols in 1895, Luna Park in 1903. Two things hold it back. First, the paper starts repeating its own punchlines word for word about twelve months in, which is when a reader is paying closest attention. Second, a few lines land on the people sold to rather than the sellers, and two template bugs (a testimonial printed under the wrong customer's name, and a woman given a wife) break the spell.

## What works

- **The aim is almost always right.** The best lines take a bureaucrat, a quack or the trade itself down by one clause:
  - "The truant officer reports two hundred boys… one was found selling a remedy, at a profit."
  - "Twelve ladies of Brooklyn have vanished entirely. _The proprietors are not responsible for vanished ladies._"
  - "Coffins, pine, $4.00. Lilies, plentiful." This footer even moves: "$4.15. Lilies, firm." in the month my customer died.
  - "The Department asks mothers not to dose them with anything that makes them quiet; the druggists ask what else there is to sell."
  - "The County Medical Society has resolved, for the eleventh year running… and adjourned to a dinner advertised in the same columns as the medicines."
  - "A druggist sued by a widow was found not liable, the jury holding that her husband had read the label… The label said 'harmless.'"
  - "CHARITY.—…The poor were not admitted, but are said to have done well out of it." (1903)
- **The letters are the best thing in the game.** All 27 fit their month and season:
  - the Fourth of July committee in June;
  - "A Question for Lent" in March 1886: "(It counts. — M. Conlon.)";
  - Miss Devlin before Easter: "would rather be interesting than not";
  - the errand boy: "I am a honest boy but I am also fourteen";
  - the diploma mill: "examination is waived in cases of evident merit, and all cases are evident";
  - the subscription history: "Gentlemen who do not subscribe are not, as a rule, leading men";
  - the sealer: "a quarter-ounce weight with ambitions".

  The poor correspondents (Cullen and the baseball, Mrs. Sheehy and the croup) are written with affection, never mocked. Every reply says what it costs and what it does before you choose. The inquest letter's pencilled odds ("about 4 in 10 for naming. —J.D.") are honest.

- **A returning writer remembered me:** Detective Lynch's follow-up ("Your till was found in Hester street, as I said it might be… your name is in it against the consideration") was the moment the post felt alive.
- **The paper agrees with the books:** 19 × 60c = $11.40 takings, correct. The callers lines read "one by the likeness on the label" and "one drawn by the gilt sign", so every letter choice shows up in the ledger.
- **The paper fits its decade:** the "TWO CENTS · NOW WITH HALF-TONES" masthead in 1895; "ONE CENT · and the Foe of Fraud" in 1903, still carrying Mrs. Whittle's ad, which is a joke in itself; heroin in THE MARKETS in 1903; League of American Wheelmen ads.
- **Look:** the proof page with "dele (strike out)" on the PERSONAL items, the skeletal Death figure at the counter, and the collapsed "Sent: …" strip on answered letters.
- **Save and resume:** a hard kill mid-month and a menu save-and-quit both brought back exactly the shop I left.

## First hour

- **0:00, title page.** The content note is unusually good, and I took its promise as my yardstick. A NEW SHOP showed the title again with CONTINUE, which confused me for a second.
- **0:02, first edition.** I read it straight through. Within three items I knew it was a Bierce-and-Twain kind of paper. I clicked an AMONG THE CALLERS item: Mrs. Lena Weber's card ("Looking for something for a teething infant"). I understood that the people in PERSONAL are my customers.
- **0:05, proof.** The tip line "Begin with the FORMULA book…" was all I needed. The assay's euphemisms ("A good, steady seller. The regulars will grow fond of it", "a risk to the frail") told me exactly what the laudanum would do. I added it on purpose, for the satire.
- **0:10.** I worked out that ad space needs $100 and a printed testimonial. The grateful letter arrived the next month and the logic clicked.
- **0:15.** The testimonial came out under the wrong name. The misspelling was clearly deliberate, the name swap clearly not.
- **0:20, May 1885.** Death appeared beside Widow Becker with a plain sentence of advice. **This is when the game clicked:** the paper is the consequence of my proof.
- **0:28, July and August 1885.** The diploma was announced ("the ink on it is very nearly dry"). Callahan died, not Becker. The inquest had honest odds, and the jury named the bottle, with "The foreman remarked that he had a bottle of it at home, and would be pouring it away." I reformulated.
- **0:35.** Quit mid-month twice, by a hard stop and through the menu. Both came back exactly.
- **0:42, November 1885.** The first verbatim repeat. By the January 1886 edition I could finish the jokes before reading them.
- **0:48.** Took the Larger Copper. It was the wrong venture for my size, and I learned that from the empty till, not the drawer.

## Score

**8 / 10.** The writing is the reason to own this: sharp, historically exact, almost always aimed upward, and the letters are better still. What would move it to 9: never reprinting a punchline within a game, fixing the testimonial name and the gender template, and rewriting the four lines that land on mothers and an immigrant.
